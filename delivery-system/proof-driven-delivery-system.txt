PROOF-DRIVEN DELIVERY SYSTEM
============================
As of 2026-11-15

This is the design and the reasoning. The manual that sessions follow is
DELIVERY-AGENT.txt, in the same directory, with its parts under manual/ and
the coordination tool at tools/dq, generated from the code blocks in
script-instructions/ (git holds no scripts).


1. THESIS
---------

Delivery is no longer limited by how much code the team can write. It is
limited by how fast the team can prove a change is right. The system below
is built around that constraint.

"Unlimited delivery" is the right ambition, but throughput will always equal
the narrowest of four things:

- Intent clarity. An agent can only close a loop against a definition of
  good that a machine can check.
- Proof capacity. Ten test tracks serve up to 300 to 600 agent sessions:
  50 engineers with as many as 6 to 12 each, once their remit has earned
  that many. That ratio is the hard cap today.
- Human judgment. 62 people decide what matters, approve what ships, and
  answer what agents cannot.
- Integration risk. Twenty repos changing in parallel collide unless changes
  are small, flagged and proven together.

Eight principles follow from that:

1. Every work item carries its own proof. No proof definition, no start.
2. Items advance on evidence, not on status updates or assertion.
3. Humans own intent and judgment. Agents own the execution loop.
4. Prove in the cheapest environment that can falsify the change.
5. Every shipped feature leaves behind the regression that guards it.
6. Atlas is the only source that binds. Everything else is context, and
   where another source disagrees with Atlas, Atlas wins.
7. Earn trust before adding sessions. One agent that can prove its work
   comes before twelve that cannot.
8. A correction made twice becomes a check. Rules live in lint, tests, hooks
   and skills, not in chat.


2. SYSTEM AT A GLANCE
---------------------

Every item climbs five rungs and ends as a permanent regression test.

Atlas binds every contract, and every finished item updates Atlas. A failed
proof at any rung sends the item back to the agent loop. The proofs of each
shipped item join the regression that guards the next change at rungs 1 to 3.


3. THE WORK CONTRACT
--------------------

The backlog item is the contract an agent executes against, so it must be
complete enough to run for hours without a human. Store it as a structured
file in a git queue repo, named by its Jira key, so any session can parse it.

Fields:

  Outcome link      The Atlas objective or North Star line this item moves
  Intent            The user-visible or business result, in two or three
                    sentences
  Scope             Repos, services and flows in play, and what is
                    explicitly out of bounds
  Remit             The remit whose lead approves, and any other remits
                    whose flows the item touches
  Depends on,       Other items that must land first, and items that wait
  blocks            on this one
  Challenge         Whether the ask moves the North Star, fits current
                    objectives, extends a legacy flow, or could be smaller
  Success criteria  For each: what is observed, the exact condition, and
                    how it is observed
  Proof scenarios   Given / when / then cases, with the faker responses
                    each one needs staged
  Proofs by rung    Which scenarios are proven at local, test track, UAT
                    and prod
  Prod signal       The metric or log signal that confirms it works live,
                    with a watch window
  Constraints       The Atlas pages that bind this work, each with its
                    status and date
  Spotlight         The spotlight that proves it locally, or "none fits"
  Autonomy tier     0 to 3, set by rule (see Guardrails)
  Budget            Maximum agent hours, iterations and track runs before
                    it must stop
  Stop conditions   What forces the agent to park and ask, beyond the
                    standard list
  Regression plan   Which scenarios become permanent tests, and at which
                    rung
  Owners            One named engineer and one named product person

Intake

A session starts from a Jira key and branches on the issue type.

  Epic      Not delivered; decomposed. One session claims the epic, maps
            its acceptance onto stories, drafts a contract for each, and
            writes proposed stories for acceptance that no story covers.
            Product creates those in Jira.
  Story,    Delivered through the ladder.
  task
  Bug       Delivered. Its first proof reproduces the bug.
  Spike     A discovery item. Its output is an Atlas decision or a set of
            contracts, not code.
  Sub-task  Part of its parent story, and delivered with it.

The ticket's Jira project and component select the remit, through a project
map in Atlas. A ticket outside the engineer's own remit follows that
remit's page and lead. A ticket that crosses remits has one primary remit,
the one whose outcome it moves.

Three checks come before any contract is drafted:

- Claim. The session claims the key first, so that no second session
  spends time on the same ticket.
- Already done? Jira status and links, open and closed items in the queue,
  git history, Atlas, and the running product. "Already satisfied", with
  the evidence, is a valid outcome and the cheapest one.
- Challenge. Does the ask move the North Star? Does it fit this period's
  objectives in the mark to market? Does it extend a legacy flow? Would a
  smaller change do? A failed check goes to product as a written
  challenge, and product answers it before anything is built. An outcome
  link that cannot be filled honestly is a failed check, not a blank to
  fill loosely.

Ready means falsifiable

An item is Ready when all of these hold:

- At least one executable proof exists, or can be written, for every rung.
- Fakers exist for every external dependency the scenarios touch.
- No open product or architecture decision blocks it.
- It fits one agent run through track proof in one to two days. Larger work
  is an epic that gets decomposed.

If an item cannot be stated as a proof, it is a discovery item. Its output
is an Atlas decision or a set of contracts, not code.

States

Draft, Ready, Claimed, Local-proven, Track-proven, UAT-proven, Live,
Confirmed, Regressed.

- Draft is an item a session has claimed whose contract is not yet agreed.
- Ready is a contract people prepared ahead that nobody has claimed.
- Claimed means the contract is agreed and the proofs are frozen.

A delivered item closes at Regressed. Three other endings exist: Satisfied
(the work was already done, or a spike was answered), Cancelled, and
Decomposed (an epic split into stories).

Each transition requires its evidence. The session's tool refuses the move
without it, and a pipeline check on the queue repo should refuse it too.

An item can also be on hold without changing state: parked, waiting for a
track, waiting for results, waiting for an approval or an answer, or under
challenge.

Who writes contracts

Sessions draft contracts from the Jira issue plus Atlas. Product and the
owning engineer correct them, and the engineer approves each one. For tiers
2 and 3 the remit lead also approves, once the proofs are written (section
5). This keeps 12 product people from becoming the first bottleneck.

Vague acceptance is turned into criteria that can be measured: a response,
a stored record, an emitted event, a screen element, an exit code, a number
with a threshold and a window. A threshold the ticket does not give is
proposed, marked as proposed, and confirmed by product or the remit lead.
It is never invented silently.


4. ATLAS
--------

Atlas already holds the right material. What it needs to drive agents is
a known format, authority, structure and a write-back loop.

Format

Atlas is written as an llm-wiki: a wiki kept for, and largely by, agent
sessions. Four things make it one:

- An index page, read first, that links to everything else.
- Pages that link to one another, each with front matter.
- An append-only log of what changed and when, so a session can catch up
  from where it last read.
- A schema page that says how the wiki is organised and how to add to it.

"llm-wiki" names this format; it is not a second source. If a separate wiki
exists under that name, it is context only, like every other source outside
Atlas, unless an Atlas decision says otherwise.

Authority

Agents must know which pages bind them and which are only context. Give
every page front matter with status (draft, agreed, superseded), owner, date
and what it supersedes. Then set a precedence order:

1. North Star
2. Principles
3. Decisions, newest wins, superseded ones marked
4. Remit pages
5. Agreed flows
6. Target state

Reference pages describe the system and do not bind: the flow map, the
catalogs and the glossary. A session trusts them when they are fresh and
checks them against the running product when they are not.

Ideas, minutes and legacy flows are context and never binding. A decision
made in a meeting counts only once it is promoted from the minutes to a
decision record. Every source outside Atlas is context too: a separate
wiki, ticket comments, chat. Where one disagrees with Atlas, Atlas wins and
the session reports the disagreement.

Freshness

A binding page binds only while its status is "agreed". A page with no
status, or older than a set age (90 days to start), is unverified: the
session checks it against the product or asks its owner before relying on
it, and says so in its report. Each contract lists the pages it relied on,
with their status and date.

Pages to add

- Service catalog. For each of the 20 repos: what it owns, its interfaces,
  how to run it, which spotlights include it.
- Faker catalog. What each faker stands in for, its staged scenarios, and
  when it was last checked against the real system.
- Spotlight catalog. Named partial clusters, mapped to the flows they can
  prove.
- Proof catalog. The regression suite indexed by flow and by rung.
- Glossary and domain model. One meaning per term.
- Lessons. Agent failure patterns, each turned into a rule or a check.
- Remit pages. One per remit: scope, current lead, what good looks like,
  exemplar contracts and bundles, session cap.
- Flow map. For each flow: how to reach it in a spotlight, how to drive it,
  its flags and data, known traps.
- Project map. Which Jira project and component belongs to which remit,
  flows and repos. It is how a session given any ticket finds the right
  context.

Mark every flow as legacy, transitional or target, so agents never extend a
legacy pattern.

How agents use it

- Atlas is cloned into every workspace from a remote published in the
  shared environment file. Per-repo agent instruction files stay thin and
  point to it.
- A session reads the index page first and the newest entries of the log,
  then the remit page, then the pages for the flows and services in scope.
- A contract links the specific pages that bind it. The agent loads those
  first.
- Every finished item proposes its Atlas changes as a merge request: flow
  updated, decision recorded, lesson added. Humans approve them like code.

Keeping it true

A scheduled agent compares agreed flows against behaviour on the baseline
test track each week and flags drift. Stale pages are worse than missing
ones, because agents will follow them.


5. THE PROOF LADDER
-------------------

A change climbs five rungs, and each rung answers a question the one below
cannot. An item moves up only when its evidence for the current rung is
attached.

Rung 1. Local
  Environment: Coder workspace, one spotlight with fakers
  Question:    Does the change do what the contract says, in isolation?
  Evidence:    Unit, contract and scenario test results; static checks
  Gate:        Automatic, plus a verifier agent

Rung 2. Test track
  Environment: Full cluster, all 20 repos, fakers
  Question:    Does it hold across services, and does anything else break?
  Evidence:    End-to-end scenarios, regression for touched flows, upgrade
               from current prod state
  Gate:        Automatic, plus human review by tier

Rung 3. Integrated UAT
  Environment: Real integrations
  Question:    Does it hold against systems we do not own, and does product
               accept it?
  Evidence:    Scenario run on real dependencies, faker drift report, full
               regression
  Gate:        Product sign-off for tiers 2 and 3

Rung 4. Prod
  Environment: Live, behind a flag or canary
  Question:    Does it work for real users without harm?
  Evidence:    Prod signal inside its watch window, guardrail metrics flat
  Gate:        Owner confirms; rollback is automatic

Rung 5. Regression
  Environment: All of the above, permanently
  Question:    Will we know if it ever breaks?
  Evidence:    Scenarios merged into the suite, Atlas flow updated
  Gate:        Automatic

Proof before code

The agent's first output is the failing proofs, written from the contract.
A proof that passes before any implementation proves nothing, so it is
fixed or dropped at this point. Then the proof list is frozen: from the
freeze on, only its pass marks may change.

For tiers 0 and 1 the session freezes the proofs itself. For tiers 2 and 3
the remit lead approves the contract and the written, failing proofs
together, and that approval is the freeze. Approval comes after the proofs
exist, never before. This is the cheapest review point in the whole system,
and it is where spec-driven becomes proof-driven.

The proof bundle

Every rung produces one standard artifact attached to the merge request:

- Contract ID and the commit of every repo involved
- Environment and version manifest
- Checks run, with results and logs
- Scenario evidence: API transcripts, and screenshots or recordings for UI
  and CLI flows
- What was not tested, and known risks

Reviewers read the bundle instead of re-deriving the work. Product reviews
the scenario evidence, not the diff. Build the bundle for fast human
reading: verdict first, then evidence a person can check at a glance.

Evidence depends on the kind of flow: the request and response transcript
for an API, the full terminal transcript for a CLI, a screenshot of each
asserted state for a UI, and a recording only where the assertion is about
sequence or motion. Text evidence lives with the item in the queue repo.
Large files go to a separate evidence branch or a shared directory, never
onto the claim branch or into a code repo, so the branches every session
reads stay small.

Jira cannot be written from a workspace. Each item therefore keeps a
ready-to-post comment, rewritten at every state change, and a mirror job or
the engineer posts it. People who watch Jira see progress and evidence
without sessions needing write access.

Keeping the proof honest

An agent that writes both the code and the tests can pass its own exam. Four
controls prevent that:

- Frozen proofs are protected. Changing one after the freeze raises a flag
  that the remit lead sees on the board.
- A state cannot move without its evidence. The tool checks that every
  proof marked passing has its saved output, and re-runs the local proofs
  before it records Local-proven.
- A separate verifier agent with fresh context tries to break the change
  before the bundle is built.
- Product holds back a few scenarios the building agent never sees, and runs
  them at UAT. A bundle therefore says "verified against the visible
  proofs", never "fully verified", and a failure at UAT is expected
  information, not an anomaly.
- Any failure found at a higher rung becomes a new test at the lowest rung
  that could have caught it.

Faker drift

A change that passes on a test track and fails in UAT usually means a faker
has drifted from the real system. Record real responses in UAT, sanitise
them, and use them to refresh the faker. Track this count; it tells you how
much to trust rungs 1 and 2.

Verification skills

A proof is only as good as the agent's ability to operate the real product.
A verification skill is a scripted tool plus instructions that lets a
session do that for one flow:

- Start the right spotlight and stage the faker responses.
- Drive the flow through its API, CLI or UI.
- Capture what happened: responses, logs, traces, screenshots.
- Return pass or fail with the evidence attached.

Each remit owns the verification skills for its flows, and they are the
first thing a remit builds. They grow by one rule: the second time anyone
checks something by hand, the check becomes part of the skill. Unit tests
and a clean build are not verification; the running flow is.

The flow map

Agents lose time rediscovering how to reach and control a feature. The flow
map in Atlas records it once per flow: entry points, the commands that
drive it, flags, required data and known traps. The product stays the
source of truth and the map is a cheap index into it. A scheduled session
re-checks the map against the baseline track and fixes what has drifted.


6. AGENT RUNTIME
----------------

Runs that last hours or days need a harness around Claude Code: a fixed
loop, standard commands, durable state and a way to ask for help.

The run loop

 1. Identify the work from its Jira key and issue type.
 2. Claim it, before reading or drafting anything.
 3. Check that it is not already done, and challenge the ask.
 4. Load the remit page, the linked Atlas pages and the repo instructions.
 5. Draft the contract. The engineer approves it.
 6. Write the failing proofs, then freeze them.
 7. Implement, run the spotlight, fix, repeat until rung 1 passes.
 8. Hand to the verifier agent. Fix what it finds.
 9. Merge the default branch in and prove rung 1 again.
10. Lease a test track, deploy the change set, run rung 2.
11. Build the proof bundle and open the merge requests.
12. Respond to review, then hand off to UAT.

Standard commands

Give every agent the same scripted tools: start a spotlight, run a proof,
request a track, deploy a change set, build a bundle, escalate. Scripts are
deterministic; prompts are not. Anything the system depends on should be a
script. Coordination is the first case: claiming, saving, taking over,
leasing and looking around ship as one tool, dq, with a self-test, so that
several hundred sessions follow one protocol and not several hundred
readings of a paragraph.

Durable state

- A run journal lives with the item in the queue repo: plan, progress,
  decisions taken, dead ends, and where to resume. A fresh session, on any
  machine, can resume from it.
- Commits are small and frequent, and every green commit and every journal
  update is pushed. What is not pushed does not exist for other sessions,
  or for the same engineer on another machine.
- The journal records the exact spotlight, faker and verification commands
  used, because a running spotlight and staged fakers live on one machine
  and must be rebuilt on the next.
- Time is measured, not felt. Every journal line carries a timestamp, and
  worked time is counted from the claim through the heartbeats, so an
  hours budget can be enforced.
- A long run checkpoints and restarts from the journal before its context
  fills.
- A change that spans repos uses one branch name everywhere plus a
  change-set manifest the track can deploy as a unit. Each item's code
  lives in its own worktrees, so sessions that share a machine do not
  overwrite each other.
- A long-lived branch catches up by merging the default branch in, never
  by rebasing: the branch is already pushed, and pushed history is not
  rewritten. Rung 1 is proven again after the merge and before any track
  run.

Keep or revert

Every attempt ends at a commit where the proofs are no worse than before.
An attempt that makes them worse is reset to the last green commit, not
patched forward. This keeps a long run from burying itself under its own
failed fixes.

Getting unstuck

- The same failure three times, a budget exhausted, or a stop condition met
  means park, not push on.
- A parked agent commits one specific question, addressed to one person,
  to the item in the queue repo, with what it tried and what it
  recommends.
- The session then releases the item and ends. One session holds one item;
  the engineer starts a fresh session for the next. The person asked
  answers by commit, and whichever session takes the item next resumes
  from its journal.

Agent roles

  Builder    Executes the contract through rungs 1 and 2
  Verifier   Fresh context; tries to break the change
  Reviewer   Checks the diff against Atlas principles and decisions
  Librarian  Proposes Atlas updates from finished work
  Triage     Turns a failed regression or prod alert into a contract

Dispatch

There is no central dispatcher. Workspaces reach only git, so git is the
queue, the lock service and the message bus. Each engineer's sessions pull
work from it.

Git as the control plane

One queue repo, beside Atlas, holds all coordination state.

  Backlog      Contracts people prepare ahead sit under ready/, named by
               Jira key. A session can also start from a bare Jira key
  Claim        Push branch claim/<key>, before anything else. The server
               rejects a second create, so the first push wins
  Card         A small file on the claim: engineer, machine, session,
               start time, last heartbeat, state, hold, and the repos,
               services and flows the item touches
  Active       One session at a time works on an item. It is named on the
  session      card, taken and released explicitly, and may be replaced
               once it has been silent too long
  Heartbeat    Every save updates it. A save is an ordinary push built on
               the latest commit; a rejected push means someone else wrote
               first, so the session fetches, merges and tries again
  State        A field on the card, moved only with its evidence
  Board        One generated file listing every open item, question, track
               and request. A pipeline job builds it, so a session reads
               one file and not hundreds of branches
  Track lease  Branch lease/track-NN holds the holder and the expiry.
               Taking and releasing are ordinary pushes to it. The branch
               is never deleted
  Escalation   One question file in the item, addressed to one person; the
               answer is a commit
  Evidence     Small text with the item; large files on evidence/<key>
  Request      A missing capability, written to the harness team
  Close        The claim merges to the default branch when the item
               reaches Regressed or another closed state. The server side
               archives the branch afterwards

Six rules keep this sound at 600 sessions:

- Protect claim/*, lease/* and the other coordination branches from
  force-push, or the locks mean nothing. Setup tests this on the real
  server.
- Every write is a plain push on top of the latest commit. Nothing is
  forced, and no session deletes a branch.
- One directory per item and one file per question, so no two sessions
  ever edit the same file.
- Coordination pushes start no pipelines. There are thousands a day.
- A lease past its expiry may be taken by anyone. A claim that has gone
  quiet is listed for the remit lead, who decides who takes it over.
- Jira stays the record of intent, read-only. Git is the record of
  execution state.

Taking over and handing over

A claim locks the item. The active session is a second, smaller lock
inside it. An engineer's own released or silent item can be resumed by any
of their sessions, on any machine. Another engineer's item changes hands
only as a recorded handover that the engineer or the remit lead agreed to.
A session that finds it is no longer the active one stops at once.

Looking around

Before it works, and again before it uses a track, a session looks at the
board: its own items; items that share its repos, services, flows or epic;
items linked to it by a dependency; sessions that have gone silent; free
tracks and the waiting list; questions addressed to its engineer. Two items
that change the same files settle their order before either uses a track,
and record it as a dependency on both cards.

Shared knowledge of the environment

Facts that are the same for every workspace are published once, in a
shared environment file beside the manual: where Atlas and the queue are,
how Jira is read, how tracks deploy and return results. Each workspace
publishes its own config as well. A session searches Atlas, that file and
the published configs before it asks its engineer anything. When a
capability does not exist at all, it files a request to the harness team
and says what stays unproven meanwhile.

The manual itself lives in a shared repo with a version. Every session
checks the version at the start and updates its copy, so several hundred
copies do not drift. Only a short core is loaded on every start; the rest
is read when it is needed.

Sessions per engineer

Six hundred builders against ten tracks would only build a queue. This is
the shape at the full cap; a remit starts at one session per engineer (see
"Earn parallelism"). Spread each engineer's sessions across roles so most
of the work happens below rung 2.

  3 to 5 sessions  Builders, one claimed item each, working at rung 1
  1 to 2 sessions  Verifiers, on a peer's items where possible
  1 session        Reviewer against Atlas
  1 session        Contract drafting from Jira and Atlas
  1 session        Librarian and triage

Cap the number of items each engineer may have waiting for a track. At the
cap, their builders switch to spotlights, fakers, verification or regression
speed, which is the work that raises the ceiling.

Earn parallelism

Twelve sessions that cannot prove their work only lengthen the review queue.
A remit raises its session cap in steps, and the remit lead decides each
step from first-pass rate and escapes:

1. One attended session per engineer, until the remit's verification skill
   drives its main flows and items land clean.
2. Three to four sessions, with a verifier on every item.
3. Six to twelve sessions, including unattended overnight runs for tiers 0
   and 1.

Improvement loops

Some work is a number, not a feature: regression suite duration, flake rate,
track reset time, spotlight start time, a latency target. Run it as a loop
with a fixed time budget per experiment: change, measure, commit if the
number improved, reset if it did not. The metric is the proof, so these
loops suit unattended overnight sessions and are the best use of builders
that are waiting for a track. Use them only where better is a single number
that is hard to game, and keep the full proof suite as a guard so the number
cannot improve by breaking something else.


7. ENVIRONMENT CAPACITY
-----------------------

Test track hours are the scarcest resource in the system, so schedule them
like one. Ten tracks is the ceiling on concurrent full-cluster proofs,
whatever the number of agents.

Track scheduling

- Tracks are leased, not owned. A lease is a branch in the queue repo that
  names the holder and an expiry. It is released by a push, and once
  expired it can be taken by anyone. Where a person hands out tracks
  instead, a session asks, and records the track it was given.
- The track pipeline should refuse a deploy from an item that does not
  hold the lease.
- A session that finds no free track goes on a waiting list everyone can
  see, does rung 1 work meanwhile, and parks the item after a set time.
- After it triggers a deploy, a session looks for the results at a fixed
  interval. Meanwhile it does only work that does not change the code
  under test. It parks after a set time, and a run with no result after a
  longer limit is a stop condition.
- Every lease starts from a reset to a known baseline: main branch images
  plus seeded data.
- Measure request-to-ready time and reset time. These set the real cycle
  time of rung 2.
- Agents need track logs and traces to debug in a closed loop. Workspaces
  are closed, so the pipeline must commit them back to git.

A starting allocation to tune from, not a recommendation to keep:

  6 tracks  Leased item proofs
  2 tracks  Baseline: continuous full regression on main
  1 track   Merge train: batched local-proven changes proven together
  1 track   Demos, exploration and incident reproduction

Batching

One change per track will not scale. Run a merge train: combine several
rung-1-proven changes on one track, prove them together, and bisect on
failure. Small flagged changes make this safe.

Spotlights

A workspace holds one cluster or one spotlight inside 28 GB, so spotlights
carry most of the load.

- Define each spotlight as code: which services, which fakers, which seed
  data.
- Map spotlights to flows. A contract names the spotlight that can prove it:
  the smallest one that includes every service its scenarios touch. When
  none fits, the scenario proofs move to the track rung, rung 1 is reported
  as partial, and the missing spotlight goes to the harness team as a
  request.
- A workspace runs one spotlight at a time, so the sessions that share a
  machine take turns under a local lock.
- The more a spotlight can falsify, the fewer track hours each item needs.
  Investing here raises the ceiling without buying tracks.

Fakers

- Version fakers and their staged scenarios alongside the code.
- A contract that needs a new external response adds the faker scenario as
  part of the work.
- Each faker has an owner and a last-verified date against the real system.

The scaling lever

When track wait time is the longest wait in the system, compare the cost of
an eleventh track with the cost of idle agents and engineers. More tracks,
smaller tracks or richer spotlights are the three ways to raise throughput.


8. ROLES
--------

Leads keep their remits. What changes is the work: people direct, verify
and decide, and leads make "what good looks like" explicit enough to grade
an agent against.

  Outcome owner (Product)
    Intent, proof scenarios, UAT acceptance, the weekly mark to market
  Item owner (Engineer)
    Their Claude sessions, as many as the remit's cap allows: one at first,
    up to 6 to 12. Every item they claim, from the claim to Regressed
  Remit lead (Senior engineer, rotating)
    What good looks like for the remit: criteria, exemplars, verification
    skills, regression health
  Harness team (Engineers)
    Tracks, spotlights, fakers, the git queue and its board, the shared
    environment file, the manual and its tool, proof tooling
  Proof steward (Engineers)
    Suite speed, flake rate, coverage by flow
  Atlas steward (Architects)
    Principles, decisions, target state, drift
  Release and reliability (Engineers)
    Rollout, flags, monitoring, rollback

A starting split of the 50 engineers, to adjust after the pilot: 30 engineers
across the remits, including their leads, 8 on the harness, 4 proof
stewards, 4 Atlas stewards, 4 on release.

Three rules hold the model together:

- Remits follow outcomes, not repos. A remit owns its flows across
  whichever repos they touch.
- A named human owns every item. Agents execute; they are never accountable.
- The harness team is the highest-leverage group. Every hour they remove
  from a track reset or a spotlight start is returned on every item.

The skill that matters most shifts from writing code to writing falsifiable
acceptance and reading a proof bundle critically. Plan training for both,
for engineers and product.

Remit leads

A remit lead is a senior engineer accountable for one remit, and the role
rotates. The lead's job is to turn judgment into something written that
agents and other engineers can be held to:

- Owns the remit page in Atlas: criteria, exemplar contracts, exemplar
  proof bundles, known traps.
- Owns the remit's verification skills, flow map entries and remit-specific
  lint rules.
- Approves contracts and proofs for tiers 2 and 3, and is the escalation
  point for questions of quality.
- Agrees handovers between engineers, and decides who takes over a claim
  that has gone quiet.
- Calibrates the verifier with examples of work that passed and work that
  should not have.
- Sets the remit's session cap and its auto-merge scope.

Rotation works because the standard lives in Atlas, not in the lead's head.
A handover is the remit page, the open escalations and the items in flight.
Rotation also spreads understanding of each remit across the senior people,
and understanding is the part that cannot be handed to agents.


9. CADENCE
----------

Monthly sprints are replaced by continuous flow with a few fixed rhythms.
Work is pulled whenever capacity frees up; meetings exist to unblock, steer
and improve the system.

  Continuous
    Agents pull Ready items. Work-in-progress limits are tied to track
    capacity, not headcount.
  Daily, 15 minutes
    Clear the open questions, blocked items, quiet claims and the track
    queue, all from the board. Async first.
  Weekly
    Mark to market: objectives against items Confirmed. Set next week's
    objectives. Check the Ready inventory covers two weeks. Promote
    decisions into Atlas.
  Monthly
    Find where items waited longest and fix that one constraint. Rebalance
    track allocation and roles.
  Quarterly
    Refresh the North Star and target state.

Two things change in substance, not just in name:

- Planning commits to outcomes, not to a sprint's worth of tickets. The
  weekly objective is a result with a proof, and the backlog reorders freely
  underneath it.
- The demo is the proof bundle. Sprint reviews go away because evidence
  arrives with every item.


10. GUARDRAILS
--------------

Autonomy is granted by tier, and the tier is set by rule from what a change
touches, never by the agent's own judgment.

Tier 0
  Change:       Tests, docs, refactors with no behaviour change, dependency
                bumps
  Before build: Nothing
  To merge:     Agent merges after rung 2; humans audit a sample
  To prod:      Normal pipeline

Tier 1
  Change:       Behaviour change in one service, behind a flag
  Before build: Nothing
  To merge:     One engineer approves the bundle
  To prod:      Owner turns the flag on

Tier 2
  Change:       Cross-service change, or any API, contract or schema change
  Before build: Remit lead approves the contract and the written proofs
  To merge:     Engineer and product approve
  To prod:      Canary, then owner confirms

Tier 3
  Change:       Data migration, auth, security, money, anything irreversible
  Before build: Human-led design; remit lead approves the written proofs
  To merge:     Full human review
  To prod:      Human-run release

Hard rules

- Agents hold no prod credentials. Prod changes go through the pipeline
  only.
- New behaviour ships flagged off. Rollback is automatic when a guardrail
  metric moves.
- Protected paths need human review whatever the tier: frozen proofs,
  pipeline config, tier rules, faker baselines, the delivery manual and
  its tool.
- No session force-pushes, pushes to a default branch, deletes a remote
  branch or rewrites pushed history.
- Every run leaves an audit trail: contract, journal, bundle, approvals,
  and a history of every state change, freeze and takeover with who and
  when.

Stop conditions

An agent parks and escalates when any of these occur:

- The same failure has survived three distinct fix attempts.
- Its budget of hours, iterations or track runs is spent.
- The work needs a repo or service outside its scope.
- The contract conflicts with an Atlas principle or decision.
- A proof scenario is ambiguous or contradicts another.
- A frozen proof appears to be wrong.
- The remit page does not say whether the result is good enough.
- A track run has produced no result within the set limit.
- The environment is broken in a way the session cannot fix.
- The next action cannot be undone.
- A stop condition particular to the item, named in its contract, is met.

When something escapes

A defect that reaches UAT or prod triggers three things: a regression test
at the lowest rung that could catch it, an entry in Atlas lessons, and a
check on whether the tier rule was too loose.

Rules live in the codebase

Correcting an agent in chat fixes one session. Encoding the correction
fixes all 600. When the same correction is made twice, it becomes one of
these, in order of preference:

1. A lint rule or pipeline check, which cannot be skipped.
2. A hook in the agent harness, for actions that must or must not happen
   every time.
3. A skill or playbook, for knowledge needed only on some tasks.
4. A line in the repo's agent instructions, kept short, for what applies to
   every task.

Earned auto-merge

Human review is the next bottleneck after tracks. The way through is to
move work down the tiers, not to review faster. A remit lead may widen what
merges without human review when the change type can be checked fully by
program and the remit's escape rate supports it. Changes that cannot be
verified by program stay with human review, whatever the volume.

Unattended runs

A run that is meant to last hours stops at the first permission prompt that
nobody is there to answer. Before a remit runs unattended:

- Every command the loop uses is approved in advance by rule: git in the
  item's worktrees, the build and test commands, the spotlight and faker
  commands, the verification skills, the Jira read commands.
- The session runs in a mode that refuses anything not approved in advance
  and carries on. It never waits for an answer.
- Hooks block a force-push, a branch deletion and a push to a default
  branch. They send a change to a protected path to a person. They keep a
  session from ending with unsaved work, or with a proof marked passing
  that has no evidence.
- The whole loop is rehearsed once in that mode before it is trusted.

Hooks and rules on a workspace are guard rails. The guarantee that a claim
cannot be overwritten is the server's branch protection.


11. METRICS
-----------

The weekly mark to market should show two things: whether outcomes moved,
and which constraint is currently limiting flow.

  Outcome
    Weekly objectives met; North Star metric
      -> Whether delivery is moving what matters
  Flow
    Items Confirmed per week; lead time from Ready to Confirmed
      -> Real throughput
    Wait time against work time at each rung
      -> Where the constraint is
  Proof quality
    First-pass rate at each rung
      -> Whether lower rungs are doing their job
    Escapes: failures a lower rung should have caught
      -> Trust in the ladder
    Prod rollbacks and faker drift count
      -> Trust in tracks and fakers
  Capacity
    Track wait time, reset time, utilisation
      -> Whether to add tracks or enrich spotlights
    Regression duration and flake rate
      -> Cost of every proof
  Humans
    Review turnaround; escalation response time
      -> Whether people are the bottleneck
  Agents
    Items finished without intervention; interventions per item; cost per
    item
      -> Whether contracts and the harness are good enough
  Backlog
    Days of Ready inventory
      -> Whether intent is keeping up with capacity

Each week, name the single longest wait and assign one change to shorten it.
That habit, more than any metric, is what moves the ceiling.


12. ROLLOUT
-----------

Convert one remit first, then scale what works. Automating an unproven loop
across 50 engineers multiplies its flaws. Each phase ends at a gate counted
in proven items.

Phase 1. Pilot contract (weeks 1 to 2)
  - One remit: its lead, about six engineers, two product
  - Contract template, Atlas authority rules, proof bundle v0
  - Queue repo with protected claim branches, track leases by hand
  - The coordination tool's self-test and protection check pass on a real
    workspace against the real server
  GATE: 3 items Track-proven, each with a bundle

Phase 2. Closed loop, one remit (weeks 3 to 6)
  - Verification skill for the remit's flows, spotlights as code
  - Verifier agent, tier rules, protected proofs
  - Full ladder to prod, with regression promotion
  GATE: 10 items Regressed, lead time measured

Phase 3. Scale to four remits (weeks 7 to 12)
  - Git queue for every remit, lease branches with reset, merge train
  - Mark-to-market dashboard and Atlas write-back
  - Tier 0 auto-merge turned on
  GATE: track wait and first-pass targets met

Phase 4. Whole organisation (months 4 to 6)
  - Every remit converted, session caps raised on evidence
  - Sprints retired, all work enters as contracts
  - Unattended multi-day runs, weekly drift checks

The timings are a proposal; the gates are what matter, and a phase does not
end until its gate is met. The other remits keep their current process
until phase 3 reaches them. After month six the work becomes the monthly
constraint review: find the longest wait and remove it.


13. OPEN DECISIONS
------------------

These need an answer before the pilot starts, because each one changes what
gets built.

[ ] Whether the llm-wiki is Atlas itself, as this document assumes, or a
    separate wiki. If it is separate: where it lives, and whether any of
    it binds.
[ ] Who posts each item's Jira comment: a mirror job with write access, or
    the engineer. Sessions prepare the comment and cannot post it.
[ ] Who approves bundles at each tier, by name. Proofs for tiers 2 and 3
    are approved by the remit lead.
[ ] Who owns the board job and the archiving of closed claims, and how
    often the board is rebuilt.
[ ] Whether tracks are leased through git, or handed out by a person.
[ ] Where large evidence goes: evidence branches in the queue repo, or a
    shared directory.
[ ] The thresholds: when a silent session may be replaced (120 minutes to
    start), when a quiet claim is listed for the remit lead (3 days), when
    an Atlas page counts as stale (90 days).
[ ] The permission mode for unattended runs.
[ ] Whether tier 0 changes may merge with no human review from day one.
[ ] How track results, logs and traces return to a closed workspace. This
    doc assumes a git push triggers the track pipeline and the pipeline
    commits results back.
[ ] How many integrated UAT environments exist and who controls deploys
    there. UAT is the likely next bottleneck after tracks.
[ ] Whether prod has feature flags, canary rollout and automatic rollback
    today, or whether those must be built first.
[ ] Who owns faker fidelity, and how real responses are captured and
    sanitised.
[ ] The budget per item in agent hours, iterations and track runs, and the
    budget for more tracks.
[ ] Which remit and which people form the pilot.
[ ] How often remit leads rotate, and the handover checklist.
